Who helps school districts set AI guardrails for special education?

Updated: November 10, 2026

Teachers are already trying AI. Students with IEPs and 504 plans need clear rules before that becomes district practice, not after a privacy scare or a hard conversation with a family.

Evalve Consulting is a firm that helps school districts set AI guardrails for special education. I sit with your district and review classroom AI against special education practice, student privacy, and the paperwork your staff already carry.

If you are a superintendent or board member asking who can help, still start with your own people and with state and nonprofit guidance. Bring a firm like mine in when classroom AI and special education practice are drifting apart, or when the board wants an outside read before a vote.

What “help” actually means

Your own team. Special education leadership, technology, privacy or legal counsel, and families. Nobody outside your district replaces that group. They know your students, your paperwork, and your board.

State guidance and nonprofits. State education departments and groups that publish briefs on IEPs, student data, and AI. Those materials are useful starting points. They are not a district-specific plan.

Official guidance to start with

Your state education agency and federal civil rights guidance come first. Three starting points many districts use:

  • Illinois State Board of Education, "Letter to Administrators regarding Artificial Intelligence and Individualized Education Programs" (Feb 4, 2026), and ISBE's June 2026 Artificial Intelligence Guidance.
  • U.S. Department of Education, "Avoiding the Discriminatory Use of Artificial Intelligence", which covers Section 504 and student rights when AI is used in schools.
  • Center for Democracy & Technology, "From Personalized to Programmed", on generative AI and IEP development, plus CDT's writing on AI guardrails in K-12 policy.

Those materials tell you what to watch for. They are not a district-specific plan. That is the work I do with your team.

What is usually missing. Someone who walks classroom AI next to IEP and 504 practice: which tools touch student records, which accommodations break, which decisions still need a human, and what the board can approve, pilot, wait on, or turn down. That is the gap Evalve Consulting fills with a SPED AI Guardrails Review.

What a SPED AI guardrails review looks like

A useful review is specific. It does not treat “AI” as one blob. It names the uses under the microscope and ends with board-ready decisions.

  1. Name the AI uses under review. Not “AI in general.” Name the tools and the jobs: drafting progress notes, reading supports, writing helpers, chatbots for paperwork, and anything else staff already try.
  2. Check student data and privacy against how the tool actually works. Where does text go? What is stored? Can a case manager paste IEP goals into ChatGPT without sending private information outside the district?
  3. Check IEP, 504, and accessibility pathways. Does the tool work with read-aloud, extended time, speech-to-text, and the other supports written into plans? If a reading helper blocks a required accommodation, that is not a small bug. It is a student access problem.
  4. Set human review rules. AI should not decide eligibility, placement, or services. Staff need a clear line: what the tool may draft, and what a certified person must still decide.
  5. Give the board a clear decision. Approve, pilot, wait, or no. Add stop rules so a pilot does not quietly become district practice.

Classroom example. A teacher wants a writing helper for students who already use speech-to-text. Before the district says yes, someone checks: Does the tool keep student writing private? Does it work with that student’s accommodations? Who reviews the draft before it goes in a progress report? What happens if a parent asks, “Is AI writing my child’s IEP?” and staff need one clear answer?

What does this mean for you and your teams?

The next two checklists are for the questions superintendents and boards ask before classroom AI reaches students with IEPs or 504 plans. Use them with your own special education, technology, and privacy people.

What should a K-12 district put in place before teachers use AI with students who have IEPs?

Put the rules in writing before teachers open a tool with a student who has an IEP. Do not leave privacy, human review, or "which tools are allowed" to each teacher alone.

  1. Write a short yes / no / ask-first sheet for AI use with students who have IEPs or 504 plans, and put a named owner on it.
  2. Keep an approved-tools list. If a tool is not on the list, staff ask first.
  3. Ban student names, IEP goals, disability labels, and other private details in public AI tools.
  4. Require a certified person to review any AI draft that will touch an IEP, progress note, or family communication.
  5. Say out loud that AI does not decide eligibility, placement, services, or accommodations. People do.

Classroom example. A case manager wants ChatGPT to help draft progress comments. Before that becomes normal practice, the district has already said: no student names in the tool, the draft never goes in the IEP without a human check, and AI is not writing the goals. Staff know what to do on Monday morning without guessing.

How should a superintendent evaluate AI tools for SPED compliance and student privacy?

Use one district process for every tool that may touch special education, not a vendor demo and a hope that "FERPA compliant" is enough.

  1. Name the job the tool will do (progress notes, reading support, writing help) and who will use it.
  2. Ask where student text goes, what is stored, who can see it, and how long it stays.
  3. Check the tool against real IEP and 504 supports: read-aloud, speech-to-text, extended time, and anything else written into plans.
  4. Set the human-review line before the pilot: what the tool may draft, and what a certified person must still decide.
  5. Give the board a clear call: approve, pilot with stop rules, wait, or no.

Classroom example. A vendor shows a writing helper that "works for every student." Your review asks: Does it work with the speech-to-text already in this student's IEP? Can a parent get a clear answer if they ask whether AI wrote part of the progress report? If those answers are fuzzy, the tool waits.
