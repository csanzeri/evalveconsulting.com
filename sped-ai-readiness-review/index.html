What a SPED AI readiness review looks at

I run it as a 7-week virtual review, the same length as my SPED AI Guardrails Review. It is specific. It does not treat "AI" as one blob. Over those seven weeks, I look at six things:

  1. Where AI is already in use. I ask your staff, not just your vendors. Which tools are case managers, teachers, aides, and related service staff opening today, and for which jobs? Progress notes, parent emails, reading supports, behavior notes, meeting summaries.
  2. Where student information goes. Are student names, IEP goals, or disability details being pasted into public AI tools? What does each tool store, and who can see it?
  3. Whether tools work with IEP and 504 supports. Does a reading helper still work with read-aloud, speech-to-text, and extended time? If a tool blocks a support written into a plan, that is a student access problem, not a small glitch.
  4. Which decisions stay with people. AI does not decide eligibility, placement, services, or goals. I check whether your staff know where that line is and who signs off on an AI draft.
  5. Where the time goes. Which paperwork eats the most hours for your special education staff? This is where the building starts, because a review that saves nobody any time does not last.
  6. What is ready and what is not. Is there a named owner, an approved tools list, a way for a teacher to ask about a new tool, and a plain answer for a parent who asks if AI wrote part of the IEP?

Classroom example. A case manager has a stack of progress reports due Friday and starts pasting notes into a public chatbot to save time. The readiness review finds that pattern before it becomes normal. Then we build a better path together: an approved tool, a short template that keeps names and private details out, and a rule that a certified staff member reviews every draft before it goes in the report.

Then we build it with you

The review is the door. The building is the point. Most districts do not need another slide deck about AI. They need a few workflows that are safe for students with IEPs and 504 plans and that save staff time this semester. After the review, I build those with your team.

  • A yes, no, and ask-first sheet with a named owner, so a teacher knows in one minute what is allowed with students who have IEPs or 504 plans.
  • A tool request path so a teacher can ask about a new tool and get a written answer after someone checks privacy and accommodations.
  • Paperwork workflows for the jobs that eat the most time, like drafting progress notes or parent letters, with student details kept out and a human check built in before anything is sent or filed.
  • Family communication steps so parent messages drafted with AI are reviewed by a person for accuracy, including translated messages.
  • A small pilot with stop rules on one team first, so a workflow is tested before it spreads, and nothing becomes district practice by accident.

I build inside the tools your district already approves when I can. Staff learn each workflow by using it on their own work, not by sitting through a training series. You keep everything we build.

Illinois starting points

Official Illinois guidance to start with

If you lead special education in Illinois, two documents from the Illinois State Board of Education are good starting points before any review:

  • ISBE Special Education Department, "Letter to Administrators regarding Artificial Intelligence and Individualized Education Programs" (February 4, 2026). It reminds districts of their duties when writing IEPs and asks them to review or revise their own AI policies and procedures so students with IEPs keep their rights.
  • ISBE, Artificial Intelligence Guidance (June 2026), written in response to Public Act 104-0399. For students in special education, it stresses adult oversight and making sure AI supports match what the IEP or 504 plan says. It also says AI-assisted family messages should get a human review for accuracy.

Those documents tell you what to watch for. A readiness review turns them into a plan for your district, and the build turns the plan into workflows your staff use.

How this is different from an audit that ends in a report

Many readiness audits end with a findings briefing and a roadmap. Those are useful, and you will get a clear written plan from me too. The difference is what happens next.

I do not hand you a report and leave. The review shows me which workflows are worth building first, and then I build them with your staff. Success is not a finished document. It is a case manager who gets an hour back on Friday without putting student privacy at risk.

How does this connect to AI guardrails?

Guardrails are the rules: what is a yes, what is a no, and what needs a check first. The readiness review shows which rules you need, and the build puts them into daily work. My guardrails page is at evalveconsulting.com/sped-ai-guardrails/.
